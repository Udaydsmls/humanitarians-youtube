## Beat 1 — AI ACCOUNTABILITY · SEC FILINGS

**On screen:** PASS / FAIL / UNKNOWN

**Narration:** Most validation is built to give one of two answers, pass or fail. I think that is a mistake, and I want to show you why. Because a lot of the time, the honest answer is neither. The information you would need to judge simply isn't there. Force that into a pass, and you have manufactured confidence out of nothing. So my tool has a third answer, and it is the one I care about most: unknown.

## Beat 2 — THE PROBLEM

**On screen:** A missing input is not a passing check.

**Narration:** Here's the trap. To check that a balance sheet balances, you need assets, liabilities, and equity. If one of them is missing, you cannot run the check. A two-answer system has to pick: call it a pass, or call it a failure. Pass is a quiet lie, you didn't actually verify anything. Fail cries wolf on data that might be perfectly fine. Neither tells the truth.

## Beat 3 — THE MECHANISM

**On screen:** Every check returns one of three verdicts.

**Narration:** So every rule returns one of three verdicts. Pass, when the data is present and the check holds. Fail, when it is present and the check is violated, an impossible margin, a balance sheet that doesn't balance. And unknown, when the inputs aren't there to judge. Unknown is not a cop-out. It is the system refusing to pretend. It surfaces exactly where you cannot yet trust the data, instead of hiding it under a green checkmark.

## Beat 4 — IN PRACTICE

**On screen:** The impossible number that dashboards missed.

**Narration:** Here's it working. A sample floating around the ecosystem reported a gross margin of three hundred and ten percent. Not merely wrong, impossible. A margin cannot exceed one hundred percent. A polished dashboard showed it anyway, because a dashboard doesn't care whether its inputs make sense. A rule three lines long fails it instantly. No model, no cleverness, just a check that knows what is arithmetically impossible.

## Beat 5 — THE TAKEAWAY

**On screen:** Trust a checker that can say ‘I don’t know.’

**Narration:** So the takeaway. The next time something validates data for you, ask whether it can say I don't know, or whether it is forced to pretend. Does it catch the truly impossible, not just the unusual? When it can't decide, does it tell you clearly? A checker that is always certain is not trustworthy. One that is honest about its own limits is. Calibrated doubt beats confident guessing, every time.
