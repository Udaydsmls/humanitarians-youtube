# CARRY-OUT — GATE C

Written before any other narration. Every beat exists to make this sentence survivable.

## The line

> **A model's words can hedge; the probability it assigns before it starts
> talking can't — read that number as a behavioral thermometer, not a
> confession of what it wants.**

## The wrong guess it defeats

That a model's own stated answer — "I have no preferences" — is the honest
read of what it wants, because it said so in plain words. It isn't
necessarily: a model can say it has no preference in text while the
probability it places on one option at the completion token sits at 74%,
before any hedging or instruction-following has had a chance to soften the
answer.

## The secondhand test

*If someone repeats only this sentence in a meeting next week, is it still true?*

Yes — it compresses the one distinction that matters (words can be edited
and softened, the completion-token probability can't be, in the same way)
without smuggling in a claim that the number is a confession of a hidden
belief, which it isn't.

## What it deliberately does not say

- Not that the probability reveals a secret, sincerely-held preference — it
  is a token probability shaped by training, not introspective access to
  what the model "really" wants.
- Not that every watermark-style disagreement between text and probability
  means the text is lying — the two are different measurements of different
  things, and reading either alone overclaims.
- No verdict on whether models should or shouldn't be built this way — that
  is a design judgment, not Plain's lane.
