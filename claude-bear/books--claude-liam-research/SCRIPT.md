## B00 — cold open (BrutalistHesitantWriter)
People assume researching a market means burying a full day in articles. It
doesn't — point Claude at the sources and that day compresses to about an
hour. So what does that hour actually get you?

## Act I — The unpaid homework

**NB01 — You already do this** (source B01)
You might not call yourself a researcher. But if you've ever spent an
afternoon reading articles to understand a market before entering it, or
pulled together everything on a competitor before a pitch — that's the work.
You've done it. It just never made it onto the calendar.

**NB02 — The afternoon that vanishes** (source B02)
Here's what it usually looks like. An afternoon vanishing into open tabs and
printouts, trying to hold a stack of sources in your head at once. Useful
work — but slow, and easy to keep postponing.

**NB03 — A day into an hour** (source B03 — WRONG-GUESS PAYOFF)
Point research support at the same pile and the shape changes. Give it a
topic and a set of sources, and you get in about an hour what used to take a
day of reading and note-taking. The bottleneck was never your intelligence —
it was your time.

## Act II — Synthesis, not summary

**NB04 — Sources in, meaning out** (source B04)
Give Claude a topic and a stack of sources — articles, reports, documents —
and it pulls out the common themes, the places they conflict, and the
takeaways that matter. Your sources flow in; structured understanding comes
out.

**NB05 — What they mean together** (source B05)
The difference that matters: it doesn't tell you what each source says, one
by one. It tells you what they mean together. A pile of separate accounts
becomes one picture — the throughline no single article stated on its own.

**NB06 — Summary versus synthesis** (source B06)
That's the line between summary and synthesis. A summary just shrinks each
source. Synthesis reads across them — and it's the version you can actually
make a decision on.

## Act III — Scouting the field

**NB07 — Four scouting jobs** (source B07)
Pointed at your market, the same engine does four jobs. Competitor
intelligence. A structured market review. Gap analysis — what's covered and
what isn't. And citation tracking, so every claim keeps its source.

**NB08 — Reading the rivals** (source B08)
Competitor intelligence first. Claude reads how rivals position themselves,
what they're building, how they talk to customers — and where the gaps sit.
It works from public information, and gets sharper if you've saved their
materials to your folders.

**NB09 — The empty quadrant** (source B09 — ANCHOR PLANTED)
What comes back is a map, not a list. Where each competitor sits on price
and focus, who's crowded together, and the corner nobody's standing in. That
empty quadrant is often the whole point of the exercise — hold onto it.

**NB10 — Every claim, sourced** (source B10)
Every finding stays attached to where it came from. Ask for proper
attribution and Claude tracks the sources and formats the citations — and
it'll hook into a reference manager if you already run one.

**NB11 — Find the hole** (source B11)
Gap analysis runs the same move on your own coverage. After reading across a
topic, Claude shows what's been handled well and what's thin — in the
market, or in your own research. You see the hole before it costs you.

**NB12 — Ask it precisely** (source B12 — composer ask converted to GRAPHIC;
NO-GENAI/NO-PANTRY LAW + WRITER LAW: the composer UI does not open mid-body)
Most of this rides on how you ask. For a competitor deep dive, you'd type
something like this: everything public on their positioning, pricing model,
audience, recent launches, and customer sentiment — then the strengths and
weaknesses. Specific in, specific out.

**NB13 — Your own shelf** (source B13)
Or point it at a batch you've already collected. Drop a folder of articles
on a topic into Cowork and ask for the trends they agree on, the places they
disagree, and what it means for you specifically. Synthesis on your own
shelf.

## Act IV — Research that decides

**NB14 — One structured briefing** (source B14)
Say you're weighing a new market. Ask Claude to research it — how big,
who's already there, what customers actually need, where the openings are —
and it returns a structured briefing: context, players, pain points,
opportunity gaps, and the things to watch.

**NB15 — Purpose shapes the output** (source B15)
But the output is only as sharp as the purpose you name. "Research AI
trends" gives you a bland overview. "Research AI trends so I can decide
whether to add AI services to my practice" gives you focused, usable
intelligence. The purpose shapes the answer.

**NB16 — The edge of a decision** (source B16 — ANCHOR PAYOFF, returns to
NB09)
This is the moment research is really for — standing at the edge of a
decision you can't take back, maybe eyeing the empty quadrant you spotted in
the competitor map. Better to have scouted the ground first than to find
out after you've stepped.

**NB17 — Options, side by side** (source B17)
For a straight choice — say, a free-trial model versus a freemium one — ask
for the advantages and drawbacks of each for your kind of product, with real
examples. Claude lays them side by side on the evidence, so you're deciding
on a comparison instead of a gut feeling.

## Act V — Leads, not verdicts

**NB18 — Leads, not verdicts** (source B18 — BOTH-DIRECTIONS, rewritten to
carry the law explicitly)
Here's the discipline: treat what comes back as leads, not verdicts. A
finding that lines up with what you expected doesn't confirm it's true — the
scout can be confidently wrong. And a finding that surprises you doesn't
confirm it's false either — it might be exactly the gap you needed to see.
Either way: verify the load-bearing claims, check the sources, and keep the
judgment yours.

**NB19 — Keep the disagreements** (source B19)
And when sources disagree, don't smooth it over. Ask Claude to flag the
contradictions and leave them standing. Experts disagreeing isn't a mess to
clean up — it's a map of where the real uncertainty lives, and that's
information you want.

**NB20 — Head down, delivering** (source B20)
Which leaves one habit worth building. Picture the operator with their head
down in delivery — shipping the work, never once looking up at the market
moving around them.

**NB21 — Head up, deciding** (source B21)
Now the one who spends an hour a month scouting — who knows their market
cold. Same desk, window open. Same effort, better decisions. That's the
whole return on the habit. Set the time aside, and let Claude do the
scouting.

## Close

**BCRY — carry-out**
Research support turns your sources into synthesis, not summary — but every
finding it hands back is a lead to verify, never a verdict to trust
outright.

**BHTF — your turn**
Your turn. Paste this into Claude: I'm considering entering a market — act
as my scout. Size it, name who's already there, map the competitors and the
gaps, tell me the three things customers actually need, and flag every
claim you're unsure about so I know what to verify. It turns this into a
scouting run on your real market — and notice it marks its own soft spots,
which is exactly where your judgment comes in.

**BOUT — outro**
Claude, Scouting — the research plugin. Liam, in for Bear.
